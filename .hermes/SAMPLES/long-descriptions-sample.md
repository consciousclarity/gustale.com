## Sample 1 — Babi Guling (Indonesia, babi-guling)

Babi guling is a Balinese whole-roasted pig. "Babi" means pig and "panggang" means grilled or roasted in Indonesian; "babi guling" is the more specific Balinese preparation, named for the turning of the spit. Outside Bali, the dish is uncommon, both because Indonesia is overwhelmingly Muslim and because the cooking technique — a whole young pig butterflied, spiced, and rotated slowly over coconut-wood embers — is uncommon outside Hindu-Balinese ceremonial contexts.

## Sample 2 — Nasi Goreng (Indonesia, nasi-goreng)

Nasi goreng is the Indonesian and Malay name for fried rice. The base preparation: pre-cooked rice stir-fried in a small amount of oil or margarine and seasoned with sweet soy sauce, shallots, garlic, ground shrimp paste, tamarind, and chilli. It is commonly served with additional ingredients — egg, chicken, prawns, vegetables. The dish is widely consumed across Maritime Southeast Asia, particularly in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, and southern Thailand, where it is part of daily meals, street-food culture, and festive cooking. Nasi goreng is distinguished from Chinese fried rice by its aromatic and smoky flavour from caramelised sweet soy sauce and powdered shrimp paste, and by being generally spicier. It is regarded as a national dish of Indonesia, and in Malaysia is officially recognised as part of the country's national culinary heritage.

## Sample 3 — Sushi (Japan, sushi)

Sushi in its most-recognised form is a small hand-pressed mound of vinegared rice paired with a slice of seafood, a piece of vegetable, or a thin omelette. This form, nigiri-zushi, is believed to have been created by Hanaya Yohei around 1824, in the Edo period (1603–1867), as a fast food for the chōnin (townsmen) class of Edo (now Tokyo). The rice is the constant: medium-grain white rice seasoned with rice vinegar, sugar, and salt — called shari or sumeshi. The topping, called neta, varies by season and by what the chef can buy at the market.

The dish has older forms. The earliest, now called narezushi, was a preservation method in which fish was fermented with vinegar, salt, and rice, after which the rice was discarded; this practice began in Southeast Asia and spread to Japan around the Yayoi period. In the Muromachi period (1336–1573), people began eating the rice as well as the fish. In the Edo period, vinegar rather than fermented rice began to be used — the step that produced the vinegared rice now central to nigiri-zushi.

A sushi meal, in the contemporary Japanese ordering, moves from lighter to heavier toppings. The rice — its temperature, its seasoning, the way it holds together when picked up — is the main thing a sushi chef is graded on. Sushi is often served with pickled ginger (gari), wasabi, and soy sauce; daikon or pickled daikon (takuan) are common garnishes. Sushi is distinct from sashimi, which is sliced raw fish or meat served without rice.
