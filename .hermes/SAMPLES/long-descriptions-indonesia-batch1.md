## 1. Babi Guling (`babi-guling`)

Babi guling is a Balinese whole-roasted pig. "Babi" means pig and "panggang" means grilled or roasted in Indonesian; "babi guling" is the more specific Balinese preparation, named for the turning of the spit. Outside Bali, the dish is uncommon, both because Indonesia is overwhelmingly Muslim and because the cooking technique — a whole young pig butterflied, spiced, and rotated slowly over coconut-wood embers — is uncommon outside Hindu-Balinese ceremonial contexts.

## 2. Bakso (`bakso`)

Bakso is an Indonesian meatball or meat-paste soup. The meatball itself is made from beef surimi, with a texture comparable to the Chinese beef ball, fish ball, or pork ball. The word "bakso" may refer to a single meatball or to the complete dish of meatball soup. "Mie bakso" is the version served with yellow noodles and rice vermicelli; "bakso kuah" is the noodle-less soup. It is sold from street carts to high-end restaurants, and frozen bakso is sold in Indonesian supermarkets. The cited source lists it, alongside soto, satay, and siomay, as one of the most popular street foods in Indonesia.

[Wikipedia contributors. Bakso — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakso, accessed 2026-10-16.]

## 3. Bubur Ayam (`bubur-ayam`)

Bubur ayam is the Indonesian and Malay name for chicken congee: rice porridge with shredded chicken, served with condiments including chopped scallion, crispy fried shallot, celery, preserved vegetables (tongcay or chai poh), fried soybean, crullers (youtiao, locally called cakwe), salty and sweet soy sauce, and sometimes a yellow chicken broth poured over the top, with kerupuk (Indonesian crackers) on the side. Unlike many Indonesian dishes, the porridge itself is not spicy; sambal is served separately for those who want heat. It is sold from travelling carts in residential streets in the morning, from warung, from fast-food chains, and from five-star hotel restaurants.

[Wikipedia contributors. Bubur ayam — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubur_ayam, accessed 2026-10-16.]

## 4. Gado-Gado (`gado-gado`)

Gado-gado is an Indonesian salad, in the Betawi and broader Indonesian sense, of raw, slightly boiled, blanched, or steamed vegetables together with hard-boiled eggs, boiled potato, fried tofu and tempeh, and sliced lontong (compressed rice cake wrapped in a banana leaf), dressed with a peanut sauce. In 2018, the Indonesian government promoted gado-gado as one of five national dishes of Indonesia, alongside soto, sate, nasi goreng, and rendang. The dish is Indonesian in its general form and specifically Betawi (the Jakarta region) in origin, and is found across the country with local variations.

[Wikipedia contributors. Gado-gado — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gado-gado, accessed 2026-10-16.]

## 5. Ikan Bakar (`ikan-bakar`)

Ikan bakar is the Indonesian and Malay name for charcoal-grilled fish or other seafood; the term literally means "grilled fish" in both languages. The dish differs from other grilled-fish preparations in the seasonings used: a flavouring paste (bumbu), sweet soy sauce (kecap manis), and sambal, with the fish wrapped in a banana leaf and cooked on a charcoal fire. The dish is found across Indonesia and Malaysia.

[Wikipedia contributors. Ikan bakar — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikan_bakar, accessed 2026-10-16.]

## 6. Martabak (`martabak`)

In Indonesia, "martabak" refers to two distinct dishes. Martabak manis is a thick sweet folded pancake based on apam balik, layered with chocolate, cheese, or sugar. Martabak telur is a pan-fried crepe folded around beaten egg, chopped leeks or scallions, and minced meat, then cut into squares and eaten with curry, pickles, or chilli sauce; it is the closer Indonesian relative of murtabak. The name "murtabak" comes from the Arabic mutabbaq, meaning "folded", and the dish is shared across the Arabian Peninsula, the Indian subcontinent, and Maritime Southeast Asia. A thinner, drier variant of martabak manis is called martabak tipis kering.

[Wikipedia contributors. Martabak — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martabak, accessed 2026-10-16.]

## 7. Nasi Campur Bali (`nasi-campur-bali`)

Nasi campur (Indonesian and Malay for "mixed rice"), also called nasi rames or sega campur in Java, is a single-scoop plate of nasi putih (white rice) accompanied by small portions of several other dishes, including meats, vegetables, peanuts, eggs, and fried-shrimp krupuk. The Balinese form, "nasi campur Bali", follows this template with a specifically Balinese choice of sides. The dish is a staple across Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, and southern Thailand, and the Indonesian and Malay form is the most widely recognised name for the "mixed rice" plate across the region.

[Wikipedia contributors. Nasi campur — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi_campur, accessed 2026-10-16.]

## 8. Nasi Goreng (`nasi-goreng`)

Nasi goreng is the Indonesian and Malay name for fried rice. The base preparation: pre-cooked rice stir-fried in a small amount of oil or margarine and seasoned with sweet soy sauce, shallots, garlic, ground shrimp paste, tamarind, and chilli. It is commonly served with additional ingredients — egg, chicken, prawns, vegetables. The dish is widely consumed across Maritime Southeast Asia, particularly in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, and southern Thailand, where it is part of daily meals, street-food culture, and festive cooking. Nasi goreng is distinguished from Chinese fried rice by its aromatic and smoky flavour from caramelised sweet soy sauce and powdered shrimp paste, and by being generally spicier. It is regarded as a national dish of Indonesia, and in Malaysia is officially recognised as part of the country's national culinary heritage.

[Wikipedia contributors. Nasi goreng — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi_goreng, accessed 2026-10-16.]

## 9. Nasi Padang (`nasi-padang`)

Nasi padang is a Minangkabau rice meal of steamed white rice served with a choice of pre-cooked dishes from West Sumatra, Indonesia, named for the city of Padang. The cited source calls it "a miniature banquet of meats, fish, vegetables, and spicy sambals eaten with plain white rice", and identifies it as Sumatra's most famous food export and the Minangkabau people's primary contribution to Indonesian cuisine. Padang restaurants are recognisable by their Rumah Gadang-style facades and the stacked rows of bowls visible through the window. Smaller Padang restaurants often carry Minang-language names. The dish is found across Sumatra, Java, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Nusa Tenggara, Papua, and in neighbouring countries including Malaysia, Singapore, East Timor, and Australia.

[Wikipedia contributors. Nasi padang — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi_padang, accessed 2026-10-16.]

## 10. Pempek (`pempek`)

Pempek, also called mpek-mpek or colloquially empek-empek, is a savoury Indonesian fishcake from Palembang, South Sumatra, made from fish and tapioca. The dish is served with a rich sweet-and-sour sauce called kuah cuka or kuah cuko, sometimes shortened to "cuko". Some Palembang residents also eat pempek with yellow noodles and diced cucumber to balance the vinegar's sourness, or add chilli powder to give the vinegar heat. The source identifies the regional name and the "cuko" dipping sauce as the main identifying features.

[Wikipedia contributors. Pempek — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pempek, accessed 2026-10-16.]
